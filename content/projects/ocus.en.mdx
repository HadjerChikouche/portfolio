## Overview

OCUS is a photography marketplace that connects global brands with a network of professional photographers for commercial shoots. I designed **AI Image Enhancement** — an internal media operations platform that rethinks quality control at scale.

The challenge wasn't aesthetic. It was systemic: how do you make AI-assisted QA feel legible and trustworthy when operators can't verify every decision manually?

---

## The Problem

### Manual QA failed on three dimensions simultaneously

The existing process asked human operators to review every image in every batch. At OCUS's scale, this broke down in three ways at once:

**Volume** — The team couldn't inspect 10,000+ images per collection without compressing review time to the point of uselessness.

**Ambiguity** — Quality decisions were subjective. Different operators made different calls on the same image. There was no shared standard, only informal norms.

**Brand consistency** — Each client had different aesthetic requirements. Without a way to encode those requirements upfront, corrections happened downstream — in revision cycles, not at submission.

## Competitive Landscape

### Eight competitors. No one owned the full pipeline.

I mapped the market against two axes: image quality assessment vs. enhancement capability. Every competitor dominated one axis. No one had built a vertically integrated QA + Enhance + Deliver pipeline. That gap was the product opportunity.

## Architecture

### A three-party decision system, not a single-screen tool

The product operated as a three-party decision system. **Operators** managed QA exceptions and protected throughput. **Brand managers** defined quality rules and evaluated business impact. **AI services** detected defects, proposed enhancements, and produced confidence-scored decisions.

The AI was not a background utility — it was an active decision-maker whose outputs shaped workflow, queue priority, and human attention. That shifted the design problem from a distributed operating model with four system boundaries.

---

## Five Architectural Decisions

Each structural choice came from weighing system performance against operational constraints discovered in research. The highest-leverage design changes were structural, not visual.

<DecisionGrid>
<Decision number="Decision 01" title="Review exceptions, not every asset">
Manual review of every image did not scale. The trade-off: limit human oversight in exchange for throughput. AI confidence scoring determines what surfaces for review — operators never see routine work.
</Decision>
<Decision number="Decision 02" title="Move brand configuration to the start of the flow">
Quality decisions depended on brand-specific thresholds, but configuration happened too late. Moving it upstream meant AI could apply the correct standard automatically, without per-image operator intervention.
</Decision>
<Decision number="Decision 03" title="Combine QA findings and enhancement actions in one surface">
Splitting review from enhancement forced context-switching. A unified surface meant faster, better-informed decisions with the full quality context visible at once.
</Decision>
<Decision number="Decision 04" title="Use presets as the unit of scale, not individual settings">
Per-image configuration made throughput and consistency impossible. Presets let operators configure once and apply across collections, reducing cognitive load and lowering error rates.
</Decision>
<Decision number="Decision 05" title="Translate operations into business metrics">
Operational teams could track processing activity, but brand stakeholders needed evidence of commercial value. Adding output-connected metrics made performance visible to the people who funded the pipeline.
</Decision>
</DecisionGrid>

## The Three Flows

### Flow 1 — Collection ingestion

The operator uploads a batch, selects a client preset, and triggers AI processing. Images route automatically based on confidence scoring — no manual triage of the full queue.

High-confidence results proceed directly. Low-confidence or ambiguous cases surface in the review queue. The operator never sees routine work.

## Interaction Model

### Built around uncertainty management, not visual polish

The interaction model had five governing principles, each derived directly from the system's routing logic and operator workflow — not from UI conventions.

**AI confidence determined information density.** High-confidence images showed minimal controls. Low-confidence images surfaced all available context, alternatives, and a commit action.

**Preview and commit were separated.** Toggling registered "pending" — not committed. Committing required explicit confirmation. This gave operators time to compare alternatives before locking a decision.

**Batch operations were the default interaction primitive.** Individual selection was secondary. The primary CTAs changed based on queue state: select all, approve selection, escalate exceptions.

**Overrides stayed explicit.** Manual intervention was reserved for edge conditions. The system never silently overrode a user action.

**Configuration reduced downstream load.** Any flexibility exposed in the interface had to pay for itself by removing decisions further down the pipeline.

---

## Three Findings That Changed the Product

**Preset timing was wrong.** Users created presets after initial selection, not before. Discovered only in usability testing — no visual refinement would have caught it. Moving preset selection earlier eliminated the primary source of configuration errors.

**KPI naming was too abstract.** Operational metrics used internal terminology that operators understood, but brand stakeholders didn't. Renaming metrics to their downstream commercial implications improved shared understanding and confidence in the system.

**AI pipeline mental model was grasped quickly.** Operators understood the AI's decision logic within the first session. The challenge wasn't AI adoption — it was preserving operator learning efficiency while maintaining contextual information density.

**120h saved** came from exception routing. Shifting human attention from universal review to ambiguous cases collapsed the inspection queue without reducing quality control.

**+24% media performance** came from co-location. Embedding enhancement decisions directly into the QA workflow drove operator adoption of AI improvements that had previously been ignored.

**+16% deliverable accuracy** came from presets. Applying brand rules consistently across collections, rather than per-operator judgment, eliminated the inconsistency that had been the primary source of rework.

**10,000 images processed** in a live pilot validated that the system handled production-scale throughput, not a controlled prototype workload.

---

## Key Learnings

**The system model mattered more than the interface layer.** Users formed mental models of the AI's decision logic within the first session. Getting the model right was higher leverage than any visual refinement.

**Presets were a strategic product primitive.** They weren't a configuration feature — they were the mechanism that made AI decisions legible and consistent across clients.

**AI trust came from decision framing, not explanation volume.** Operators didn't need to understand how the AI worked. They needed to understand what the AI was deciding, and when to override it.

**The highest-leverage changes were architectural.** Moving preset selection earlier, embedding enhancement in QA, routing by confidence — none of these were UI decisions. They were structural choices that visual design then made executable.
