## At a glance

**The challenge.** OCUS's quality control relied on manual review of every image. At marketplace scale, this created review bottlenecks, inconsistent standards across clients and operators, and slower time-to-publish.

**My role.** As UX Researcher and UX Designer, I was responsible for usage analysis and product specifications, and I designed the review workflow. My work covered user interviews and usability testing, KPI analysis, and contributing to backlog prioritization.

**Team.** A cross-functional team of a Product Manager, a Senior Product Designer, a UX Designer, data analysts and engineers.

**The approach.** We moved QA from universal review to exception-based routing. AI confidence scores decide which images need a human eye, so operators focus only on the ambiguous cases.

**Impact.** 120 hours of delivery time saved (20% below the manual baseline), +24% CTR and +16% deliverable accuracy, validated on 10,000 images in a live pilot with Uber Eats France.

---

## Context

OCUS is a photography marketplace that connects brands with a network of professional photographers. Its clients (food delivery, real estate and eCommerce) depend on image quality to drive conversion.

We designed **AI Image Enhancement**: a B2B media operations product that makes quality decisions at marketplace scale. My work covered research, specifications and the design of the review workflow.

---

## The Problem

### Manual QA failed on three dimensions simultaneously

Images came from distributed photographers, under variable lighting, with uneven adherence to brand standards. The original workflow relied on manual QA and reshoots. At scale it failed three ways at once:

**Volume** — Tens of thousands of images created review bottlenecks that no team could clear.

**Consistency** — Quality standards were applied unevenly across clients and operators, without shared enforcement rules.

**Speed** — Manual inspection delayed time-to-publish and increased the operational cost per image.

<Callout>The product problem was not "improve image editing." It was to build a system that could make quality decisions at scale without forcing humans to inspect every asset — increasing automation enough to remove throughput limits, while preserving enough control to satisfy brand and operational stakeholders.</Callout>

## Competitive Landscape

### No one owned the full pipeline

We mapped the imagery market against two axes: image quality assessment vs. enhancement capability. Every competitor dominated one axis. No one had built a vertically integrated QA + Enhance + Deliver pipeline. That gap was the product opportunity.

| Competitor | Type | Core capability | Gap vs OCUS |
|---|---|---|---|
| Clarifai | Image | Architectural style ID, quality assessment & enhancement benchmark | No workflow layer; API-only, no operator UI |
| Photoroom | Image | Cropping, blur, angle change, collage, in-painting, watermark/logo/human removal | Consumer-focused; no B2B QA pipeline or brand preset system |
| Meero / Autoretouch | Enrichment | Customizable guidelines, alignment fixes, color palette detection, user ratings | Photography platform first; no AI QA flagging for food/eCommerce at scale |
| RestB | Enrichment | Real estate image recognition and tagging | Vertical-specific; not extensible to food or eCommerce |
| Previsite / Nodalview | Image perf · Enrichment | Image performance metrics + enrichment layer for real estate | Narrow vertical; no food delivery or eCommerce coverage |
| Foxy AI / Imagen | Image | Image generation and basic quality scoring | Generation-first; no brand guideline enforcement or rejection workflow |

<Callout>Photoroom was the closest technical parallel, but it served individual creators, not brand ops teams managing 10,000+ images per month. The moat was the workflow — brand presets → AI pipeline → human gate → delivery — not any single AI capability.</Callout>

## Architecture

### A three-party decision system, not a single-screen tool

**Operators** managed QA exceptions and protected throughput. **Brand managers** defined quality rules and evaluated business impact. **AI services** detected defects, proposed enhancements and produced confidence-scored decisions.

The AI was not a background utility — it was an active decision-maker whose outputs shaped workflow, queue priority and human attention. That turned the design problem into a distributed operating model with four system boundaries: **Ingestion** (assets and metadata), **Decisioning** (AI quality analysis and enhancement proposals), **Resolution** (human approval, override or rejection) and **Reporting** (throughput and business outcomes).

## Five Decisions, Each With an Explicit Trade-off

The highest-leverage design changes were structural, not visual. Each came from weighing system performance against operational constraints discovered in research.

<DecisionGrid>
<Decision number="Decision 01" title="Review exceptions, not every asset">
**Context.** Manual review of every image did not scale operationally.

**Trade-off.** Lower universal human oversight in exchange for far higher throughput.

**Result.** Human attention shifted to ambiguous cases; batch processing became the default operating mode.
</Decision>
<Decision number="Decision 02" title="Move brand configuration to the start of the flow">
**Context.** Quality decisions depended on brand-specific thresholds, but configuration originally happened too late.

**Trade-off.** An added upfront choice in exchange for cleaner downstream automation.

**Result.** Less processing rework and more consistent rules — assets entered the pipeline with the correct decision policy attached.
</Decision>
<Decision number="Decision 03" title="Combine QA findings and enhancement actions in one surface">
**Context.** Splitting defect review from enhancement approval forced operators to reconstruct context across screens.

**Trade-off.** A denser interface in exchange for faster, better-informed decisions.

**Result.** More trust in AI enhancement proposals — operators could compare the defect, the recommendation and its impact in one place.
</Decision>
<Decision number="Decision 04" title="Use presets as the unit of scale, not individual settings">
**Context.** Per-job configuration made standards inconsistent and created high setup overhead.

**Trade-off.** Less one-off flexibility in exchange for repeatability across clients and operators.

**Result.** Standardized quality enforcement across collections and lower cognitive load to launch processing.
</Decision>
<Decision number="Decision 05" title="Translate operations into business metrics">
**Context.** Operational teams could see processing activity, but brand stakeholders needed evidence of commercial value.

**Trade-off.** Additional reporting complexity in exchange for clearer product ROI.

**Result.** AI decisions and QA throughput connected to conversion-relevant media metrics, visible to the Check Signer persona.
</Decision>
</DecisionGrid>

## The Three Flows

### Flow 1 — Collection ingestion

The operator uploads a batch, selects a client preset, and triggers AI processing. Images route automatically based on confidence scoring — no manual triage of the full queue.

High-confidence results proceed directly. Low-confidence or ambiguous cases surface in the review queue. The operator never sees routine work.

### Flow 2 — AI-mediated review

The operator opens a collection where AI decisions have already been applied. High-confidence results stay low-friction; ambiguous cases are surfaced. Detected issues and enhancement proposals sit together in one decision context: in the QA checklist a filled checkbox means a violation was detected, and enhancements stay "proposed" until the operator commits them.

<Callout>The operator shouldn't be reviewing AI outputs. They should be reviewing exceptions — the cases where AI uncertainty is high enough to warrant human judgment.</Callout>

Approved assets move to final delivery. Rejected assets enter recovery. Two dashboard surfaces serve different time horizons and different actors — a real-time view for operational monitoring, a reporting view for client-facing metrics.

## Interaction Model

### Built around uncertainty management, not visual polish

The interaction model had five governing principles, each derived from the system's routing logic and operator workflow — not from UI conventions.

**AI confidence determined information density.** High-confidence images showed minimal controls. Low-confidence images surfaced all available context, alternatives and a commit action.

**Preview and commit were separated.** Toggling registered "pending", not committed. Committing required explicit confirmation, giving operators time to compare alternatives before locking a decision.

**Batch operations were the default interaction primitive.** Individual selection was secondary. The primary actions changed with queue state: select all, approve selection, escalate exceptions.

**Overrides stayed explicit.** Manual intervention was reserved for edge conditions. The system never silently overrode a user action.

**Configuration reduced downstream load.** Any flexibility exposed in the interface had to pay for itself by removing decisions further down the pipeline.

## Validation: Three Findings That Changed the Product

Usability testing checked the flows against how operators actually worked. Each finding led to a specific change.

| Finding | What we discovered | What we changed | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Preset timing was wrong** | Users expected client context before upload, not after ingestion. | Moved preset selection to the front of the workflow. | Reduced rework and aligned the system with how operators framed collections mentally. |
| **KPI naming was too abstract** | Operational metrics were visible, but labels did not communicate business meaning clearly to stakeholders. | Reframed metrics and added contextual explanations where interpretation was ambiguous. | Improved stakeholder confidence in reporting outputs. |
| **The AI pipeline mental model was grasped quickly** | Users could distinguish approved, enhanced and rejected outcomes without additional conceptual scaffolding. | Leaned into explicit state visibility rather than adding instructional UI. | Kept the workflow compact and preserved scanning efficiency for operators. |

<Callout>Moving preset selection earlier — discovered only in usability testing — had a larger impact on task completion than any visual refinement. Research revealed the mental model; testing revealed where it broke down.</Callout>

## Key Learnings

**The system model mattered more than the interface layer.** Performance gains came from routing logic, confidence-based review and operating constraints — not from surface-level UI changes. The architecture was the product.

**Presets were a strategic product primitive.** They turned brand rules into a reusable control layer that scaled across clients and collections. The business model depended on this — and it emerged from design, not from engineering.

**AI trust came from decision framing, not explanation volume.** Users adopted the system when they could see where AI acted autonomously, where humans kept control, and how exceptions were handled.

**The highest-leverage changes were architectural.** Moving configuration earlier and restructuring decision surfaces had more impact than incremental screen refinements. Design at the flow level outperformed design at the component level.
